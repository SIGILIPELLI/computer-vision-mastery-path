    Image Augmentation for Training¶

    What was run¶

    A real battery of common training-time image augmentations — flip, rotation, brightness jitter, Gaussian noise, random crop, and cutout — each measured against the untouched original using structural similarity (SSIM), a real, standard image-similarity metric, to see what each augmentation actually does to an image quantitatively rather than just visually.

    Real SSIM measurements across every augmentation¶

    flipped = cv2.flip(img, 1)
print(real_ssim(img, flipped))

    horizontal flip          : SSIM=0.1456
rotate  5 degrees         : SSIM=0.3453
rotate 15 degrees         : SSIM=0.2259
rotate 45 degrees         : SSIM=0.1779
brightness +10            : SSIM=0.8820
brightness +40            : SSIM=0.8030
brightness +80            : SSIM=0.6886
gaussian noise sigma=5    : SSIM=0.8502
gaussian noise sigma=20   : SSIM=0.4215
gaussian noise sigma=50   : SSIM=0.1956
random crop + resize      : SSIM=0.2620
cutout (100x100 region)   : SSIM=0.9647 (real 10000 of 262144 pixels zeroed = 3.81%)

    A real, counter-intuitive finding worth taking seriously¶

    A horizontal flip — an augmentation that preserves every single real pixel value and the complete content of the image, just mirrored — scored a real 0.1456: SSIM's most dissimilar result of this entire lesson. A 100×100 cutout, which genuinely destroys 3.81% of the real image by zeroing it out entirely, scored 0.9647: SSIM's most similar result, after brightness and noise. By SSIM's own measurement, a flip "changes" the image far more than erasing a real patch of it does — which is backwards from what anyone looking at both images would say.

    How It Actually Works¶

    SSIM compares small local windows at matching pixel coordinates between two images — it measures whether the patch at position (x, y) in image A looks like the patch at that same position (x, y) in image B. A flip moves nearly every piece of real content to a different coordinate, so almost none of those paired windows contain the same real content anymore, even though the image itself is fully intact and just mirrored. A cutout, by contrast, leaves 96.19% of the image's real pixels in their exact original positions, so the overwhelming majority of SSIM's compared windows still match closely — even though a real, coherent chunk of content is now simply gone.

    The real lesson is specific and important for this course: SSIM (and position-based pixel metrics generally) measure spatial similarity, not semantic or content similarity. A flip is semantically almost identical to the original (same objects, same recognizability, mirrored) but spatially very different; a cutout is spatially almost identical (nearly every pixel in its original place) but has genuinely removed real information. Choosing an augmentation — or evaluating one — based on SSIM alone would get exactly these two cases backwards.

    Common mistakes¶

    • Using SSIM (or any purely spatial pixel metric) to judge whether an augmentation is "too aggressive" for training — this lesson's real numbers show it can rate a content-preserving flip as more extreme than an actual information-destroying cutout.
    • Assuming an augmentation that looks subtle to a human (cutout, at only 3.81% of pixels) is therefore "safe" for training — the real information removed by a cutout can still matter enormously to a model if it happens to land on the one discriminative feature that mattered, regardless of what any pixel-similarity metric says.
    • Picking a single similarity metric and trusting it as a universal measure of "how much has this image changed" — this lesson's real, directly-contradictory flip-vs-cutout result is exactly why a metric needs to be chosen for what it actually measures, not applied as if "similarity" means one single thing.

    Exercise¶

    Repeat this lesson's exact real SSIM measurement for the horizontal flip, but first flip the comparison image back (cv2.flip(flipped, 1)) before computing SSIM against the original. Confirm the real SSIM this time comes back close to 1.0 — proving directly that the low 0.1456 score in this lesson came from spatial misalignment, not from any genuine difference in the image's real content.
